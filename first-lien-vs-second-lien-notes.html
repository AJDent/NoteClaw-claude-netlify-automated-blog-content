First Lien vs. Second Lien Notes: The Position Is the Whole Deal

A second lien can pay a fatter yield than a first. Whether that extra yield is a premium or a warning comes down to one number most people never run.

AJ
AJ Dent · Take Notes Capital

Two notes cross your desk the same week.

Same kind of house. Same state. Same borrower profile on paper. One pays a yield that looks reasonable. The other pays close to double.

Most people stop reading right there and pick the bigger number. You know how that goes.

Then they find out what they actually bought.

A mortgage note is not one thing. It is a claim, and claims stand in line. Where your claim stands in that line decides what you control, when you get paid, and whether you get paid at all. Everything else about the deal is small next to that.

What Lien Position Actually Means

When somebody borrows against a house, the lender records a security interest against the property. If there is more than one loan on that house, those interests rank against each other. That ranking is lien position.

Cornell's Legal Information Institute puts it about as plainly as it can be put: a junior lien is "a security interest on a property that is subordinate to another security interest," and the senior lien "is paid back in full prior to payment of the junior lien." Same source adds the part that stings: if the borrower goes insolvent, "the junior lien will only receive payment once the senior lien has been paid back in full."

In practice, first position is usually the purchase money mortgage. The loan that bought the house. Second position is usually a HELOC, a home equity loan, or a seller carryback that got recorded after.

Same house. Same borrower. Completely different asset.

First In Time, First In Right

The default rule is recording order. California codified it back in 1872 and never changed the language: "Other things being equal, different liens upon the same property have priority according to the time of their creation" (California Civil Code section 2897). Most states run some version of the same idea.

Note the hedge built into the statute itself. Other things being equal. They are not always equal. Subordination agreements can move a lien. Some liens, like property taxes and certain association assessments, get statutory priority in some states no matter when they were recorded. That is state law, so read the state before you assume the recorded order is the final order.

But absent one of those wrinkles, the order on the title report is the order at the pay window.

What the First Lien Actually Controls

People describe first position as "safer." That undersells it. First position is not just safer, it is in charge.

  • It drives the foreclosure. The first lienholder decides whether to start, when to start, and what to bid.
  • It sits at the top of the waterfall. Refinance, sale, short sale, foreclosure auction. The first gets made whole before anybody behind it sees a dollar.
  • It can protect its own collateral. Advance for delinquent taxes, force-place insurance, add it to the balance. The first spends money to defend the asset and gets that money back first too.
  • It can restructure. Modify the rate, extend the term, capitalize arrears, take a deed in lieu. Most of the good workout options live in first position.
  • It ends up owning the house if nobody outbids it at the sale.

The first lien does not just get paid first. It decides when everybody gets paid.

What the Second Lien Actually Owns

A second position note is a claim on whatever is left after the first is made whole. That is the entire asset. Not a discount. Not a slightly riskier version of the same thing. A claim on the leftovers.

Which means the second position outcome is closer to binary than most people expect. If the senior forecloses and the sale proceeds do not reach you, your lien on that property is gone. Not reduced. Gone. LII notes the one procedural protection here: the junior lienholder "must be made party to the action or their rights will not be extinguished." Proper notice is what wipes you out. Sloppy notice is what accidentally saves you, and that is not a plan.

The debt itself may survive as an unsecured claim against the borrower depending on state law, and plenty of states restrict deficiency claims after a foreclosure. An unsecured claim against somebody who just lost their house is not the collateral you underwrote anyway.

Now the part nobody puts on the tape. A second lienholder does have real remedies. You can cure the first and keep it current to protect your position. You can foreclose your own lien. But a buyer at your sale takes the property subject to that first mortgage, which is still sitting there. So your remedy costs money you have not committed yet, on somebody else's loan, on somebody else's schedule.

The first lien holds the steering wheel. The second holds a receipt.

The Cushion Test: Illustrative Math

Here is the number people skip. Not the yield. The equity standing behind your position.

Two hypothetical seconds, both on a house worth $300,000.

 Thin SecondDeep Second
Estimated value$300,000$300,000
First lien balance$210,000$120,000
Second lien balance$30,000$40,000
Combined LTV80%53%
Equity behind your position$90,000$140,000
Value drop that wipes you outAbout 30%About 47%

Both are second position notes. Both would get called "a second" on a tape. They are not remotely the same risk.

Run the thin one through a 30% value move and the house is worth $210,000, which is exactly the first lien balance. Your $30,000 claim is now standing on nothing. Meanwhile that same first lien in that same scenario is still fully covered at par. One event, two completely different outcomes, and the only difference is where you stood in line.

And 30% is the generous version, because a foreclosure sale is not a retail sale. Take your costs, your carrying time, and a forced-sale haircut off the top before you call yourself covered. That is the whole idea behind a safety margin in note investing, and it is why LTV is the number that actually matters whichever position you are buying.

Rounded, hypothetical figures used to show the mechanics. Not a deal, not a forecast, and not a return anybody is promising you.

Where the Market Sits Right Now

Two things are happening at the same time, and they point in opposite directions.

First, more second liens are getting created. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's Q2 2026 Household Debt and Credit Report has HELOC balances rising by $13 billion to $459 billion, which is $142 billion above the low they hit in Q1 2022. Total mortgage balances came in at $13.1 trillion. So the supply of junior paper is growing.

Second, the cushion behind that paper is thinning. According to ATTOM's Q2 2026 U.S. Home Equity and Underwater Report, 41.1% of mortgaged residential properties were equity-rich, meaning a loan-to-value ratio of 50% or lower. That is down from 43.3% in Q1 2026 and 47.4% in Q2 2025. Seriously underwater properties, at 125% LTV or above, sat at 3.2%, up from 2.7% a year earlier.

More seconds being written. Fewer houses with deep equity behind them. That is the setup where a lot of thin junior paper ends up in front of buyers who are shopping by coupon.

The distress side backs it up. Federal Reserve data on FRED shows the delinquency rate on single-family residential mortgages at commercial banks at 1.86% in Q2 2026, up from 1.78% a year earlier. ATTOM counted 227,548 U.S. properties with foreclosure filings in the first half of 2026, up 21% year over year, with 27,983 completed bank repossessions, up 33%.

And here is the number a junior lienholder should sit with. ATTOM reports that properties foreclosed in Q2 2026 had been in the foreclosure process for an average of 563 days. That is the lowest since 2013, and it is still roughly a year and a half. A year and a half of watching a process you do not control, on a clock you do not set.

When a Second's Extra Yield Actually Pays for Itself

By all means I am not saying seconds are bad paper. People make real money in second position. They just make it on purpose.

The ones who do it well can check every one of these before they wire:

  1. The cushion is deep and it is your number. Not the seller's value, not the tape's value. Your own work on what that house is worth in a bad month.
  2. The first lien is verified. Current balance, payment status, escrow, whether it is advancing, whether it has already been modified. A second is only as good as the loan in front of it, so you have to underwrite that loan too even though you are not buying it.
  3. There is real pay history. On both loans, not just yours.
  4. Reserves are parked to cure the first. And the number was decided before purchase, not discovered after a default notice.
  5. You know the state. Timeline, judicial or nonjudicial, and what that state allows after a sale.
  6. The position is sized so a zero does not hurt you. Because in second position, zero is a live outcome, not a tail scenario.

Miss a few of those and that extra yield stops being a premium. It is a warning label you decided to read as a coupon. If you want the fast version of that filter, it is the same instinct behind spotting a bad note deal quickly.

The Spread Is Not Free Money

This is where I think most people get it wrong, and it took me a while to see it clearly.

A first lien and a second lien do not just have different amounts of risk. They have different shapes of risk.

A first lien tends to lose money in slices. Value slips, timeline drags, costs pile up, you take a haircut. Ugly, survivable, and mostly something you can model.

A second lien tends to lose money all at once. It is fine, it is fine, it is fine, and then a senior foreclosure closes and the position is at zero.

Those two things cannot be compared by yield alone, because yield is one number and it does not describe shape. When you buy a second at a wider spread, that spread is paying you for four separate things: a genuinely binary downside, no control over the outcome, somebody else's timeline, and capital you have to keep on standby instead of deployed.

So the real question is not "is the bigger yield better." It is simpler and harder than that.

Does this spread pay me enough for an outcome I cannot steer?

Sometimes it does. A deep second on a low combined LTV with a clean, verified first can be a genuinely good piece of paper. Sometimes it clearly does not, and the tell is almost always the same: the yield went up but the cushion did not.

The Bottom Line

Position first. Then price. Then yield. In that order, every time.

Buying a first lien means buying control. You decide the timeline, you sit at the top of the waterfall, and your downside arrives in pieces you can see coming. Buying a second means buying a claim on the leftovers, at a wider spread, with your fate attached to a loan somebody else is servicing.

Neither one is wrong. Buying the second without pricing what you gave up is.

So which one are you actually shopping for right now, the control or the coupon?
