Why Use a Note Servicer? The Unglamorous Job That Keeps You Legal

Servicing is the least exciting part of note investing and the fastest way to turn a passive asset into a compliance problem you never signed up for.

AJ
AJ Dent · Take Notes Capital

Nobody gets into notes because they are fired up about servicing.

You get in for the payment. Somebody owes $60,000 at 8% on a 30 year note, sends roughly $440 a month, and that lands while you are asleep. Round, illustrative numbers, but that is the whole pitch. No tenant. No toilet. No 11pm phone call about a water heater.

Then a reasonable sounding thought shows up. Why pay a company to collect a payment that is already coming? Set up a Zelle, keep a spreadsheet, pocket the fee.

I get it. It looks like free money on the table.

It is also the fastest way to take a passive asset and staple a part time job to it, with federal and state legal exposure attached. The law does not particularly care that you are one guy with three notes.

So here is what a servicer actually does, what rules attach the second you take a payment yourself, and what it costs. Some of this is less scary than you expect. The scary part is not the part most people worry about.

"It's Just a Check Every Month" Is Doing a Lot of Work in That Sentence

Collecting a mortgage payment is not like collecting rent. Rent is a contract between you and a tenant. A mortgage payment is a regulated transaction wrapped in about four federal rulebooks and fifty state licensing regimes, and it stays regulated no matter who is holding the note.

Here is the thing people miss. The rules do not attach to the size of your operation. They attach to the act of receiving the payment. Montana's statute spells it out about as plainly as a statute can. Under Montana Code Annotated section 32-9-103, a "mortgage servicer" is an entity that "engages, for compensation or gain from another or on its own behalf, in the business of receiving any scheduled periodic payment from a borrower pursuant to the terms of a residential mortgage loan."

Read that again. Or on its own behalf. Servicing your own loan is still servicing. There is no "but I own it" carve out in there.

What a Licensed Servicer Actually Does All Day

Most investors picture a servicer as an expensive mailbox. It is closer to a compliance department that also happens to move money.

The short version:

  • Takes and posts the payment on time. Under Regulation Z, a servicer cannot fail to credit a periodic payment "as of the date of receipt" (12 CFR 1026.36(c)(1)(i)). Sitting on a payment for five days because you were on vacation is not a neutral act.
  • Runs the escrow account. RESPA caps the cushion at "one-sixth (1/6) of the estimated total annual disbursements" and requires an annual escrow statement within 30 days of the computation year's end (12 CFR 1024.17). Taxes and insurance have to get paid, on time, from the right pot.
  • Answers written complaints on a clock. A notice of error has to be acknowledged within five business days and resolved within 30 days for most errors, seven days for a payoff error, with one 15 day extension available (12 CFR 1024.35).
  • Produces a payoff statement fast. No more than seven business days after a written request (12 CFR 1026.36(c)(3)). Try hitting that while you are at your W-2.
  • Handles the transfer paperwork. When servicing moves, the old servicer notifies the borrower at least 15 days before the effective date, the new one no more than 15 days after (12 CFR 1024.33(b)(3)). For 60 days after, an on-time payment sent to the old servicer "may not be treated as late for any purpose" (12 CFR 1024.33(c)(1)). Note buyers ding a borrower's credit in month one over exactly this.
  • Keeps the record that proves all of it. This is the actual product. Not the collection. The evidence.

None of that is hard on its own. All of it is hard to do perfectly, every month, across every loan, forever, as a side project.

"I'm a Small Servicer, So I'm Exempt." Partly True, and That's the Trap.

Here is the honest part, and it is the part most articles quietly skip.

There is a real small servicer exemption, and most note investors qualify for it. Regulation Z defines a small servicer as one that services "5,000 or fewer mortgage loans, for all of which the servicer (or an affiliate) is the creditor or assignee" (12 CFR 1026.41(e)(4)). When you buy a note, you become the assignee. So if you own everything you service and you are nowhere near 5,000 loans, you are in.

Being a small servicer gets you out of a genuinely heavy stack. Under 12 CFR 1024.30(b), sections 1024.38 through 1024.41 do not apply to you. That is the servicing policies and procedures rule, early intervention live contact by day 36, continuity of contact, and the full loss mitigation review machinery. You are also exempt from mailing a monthly periodic statement under 1026.41.

That is a real exemption. So why does it not solve the problem?

Because the exemption is narrow in exactly the places that hurt. Still fully on you as a small servicer:

  • The 120 day rule. 12 CFR 1024.30(b) carves out section 1024.41(j), which says a small servicer "shall be subject to the prohibition on foreclosure referral in paragraph (f)(1)." And (f)(1) says a servicer "shall not make the first notice or filing required by applicable law for any judicial or non-judicial foreclosure process" unless the loan is more than 120 days delinquent. Referring too early is a violation whether you service five loans or five million.
  • Escrow administration (1024.17). Not exempt.
  • Error resolution and information requests (1024.35, 1024.36). Not exempt.
  • Servicing transfer notices (1024.33). Not exempt.
  • Prompt crediting and payoff statements (1026.36(c)). Not exempt.

So the small servicer exemption frees you from the paperwork and leaves you holding every rule with actual teeth. That is a bad trade to build a business on.

And the teeth are sharp. RESPA gives the borrower a private right of action: actual damages, plus up to $2,000 more if the court finds a pattern or practice of noncompliance, plus your borrower's attorney fees (12 U.S.C. 2605(f)). On the regulator side, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's civil penalty tiers under 12 U.S.C. 5565(c)(2) run $5,000 per day for a violation, $25,000 per day for reckless, and $1,000,000 per day for knowing. Those base figures are inflation adjusted, and 12 CFR 1083.1 currently puts them at $7,217, $36,083, and $1,443,275 per day.

Per day. On one note, to save a monthly fee smaller than your phone bill.

On a Non-Performing Note, There Is an FDCPA Trapdoor

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act mostly targets third party collectors, not creditors collecting their own debts. That feels like it should protect you. Look at the actual exclusion though. Under 15 U.S.C. 1692a(6)(F)(iii), the "debt collector" definition excludes someone collecting a debt that "was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person."

Flip it. If the loan was in default when you obtained it, that exclusion does not cover you.

Which describes the entire non-performing note business. You buy the loan precisely because it stopped paying. So the moment you start calling that borrower yourself, you have walked into a statute built around call time windows, validation notices, communication restrictions, and a private right of action, all pointed at you personally.

If you are working a non-performing note toward a resolution, this is not a footnote. It is the reason experienced buyers route every borrower contact through a licensed servicer or a licensed collection shop and keep their own phone out of it entirely.

State Licensing Is a Whole Separate Problem

Everything above is federal. States run their own licensing regimes on top, and they are not uniform.

Minnesota is a clean example of how tight the door can be. Minnesota Statutes section 58.04 says no person shall engage in activities that fall within the definition of "servicing a residential mortgage loan" without first obtaining a license from the commissioner. There is an exemption list, and one of the entries covers "a person servicing loans made with its own funds, if no more than three such loans are made in any 12-month period."

Sit with that. Loans made with its own funds. A note you bought from a bank was not made with your funds, it was originated by somebody else and assigned to you. And even for loans you did fund yourself, the exemption caps out at three in twelve months.

Now multiply that across a portfolio in six states, each with its own definition, exemption list, bond and net worth requirements, and renewal calendar. A national servicer already carries those licenses. That is a large part of what you are buying.

I am not saying you can never be compliant on your own. I am saying a licensed multi-state servicer is a business, and it is a different business than the one you were trying to get into.

If the Note Sits in an IRA, Self-Collecting Gets Worse

If you are buying notes inside a self-directed IRA, the servicing question stops being about consumer protection law and becomes a tax question.

The asset belongs to the IRA. Every dollar of that payment has to land back in the IRA, and the work involved has to stay at arm's length from you personally. Section 4975(c)(1) of the Internal Revenue Code defines prohibited transactions to include the "furnishing of goods, services, or facilities between a plan and a disqualified person," the "transfer to, or use by or for the benefit of, a disqualified person of the income or assets of a plan," and any act by a disqualified person who is a fiduciary "whereby he deals with the income or assets of a plan in his own interest or for his own account."

The penalty for getting that wrong is not a fine. Under 26 U.S.C. 408(e)(2), if the individual for whose benefit the IRA was established engages in a prohibited transaction, the account "ceases to be an individual retirement account as of the first day of such taxable year," and the whole thing is treated as distributed at fair market value on that day.

The entire account. Not the one note.

Whether a specific setup crosses that line is a facts and circumstances question for your CPA and your custodian, not for a blog post. But it is why nearly every self-directed custodian wants a third party servicer in the chain, remitting straight to the IRA. It takes the argument off the table before anybody has to make it.

What It Costs, and What You Are Actually Buying

Now the number everybody wants.

Pricing varies by servicer, loan status, and state, so pull current fee schedules yourself instead of trusting a range you read somewhere. The shape is almost always the same: a one time boarding fee per loan, a recurring monthly fee per loan, then add-ons for things like escrow analysis, a modification, or a demand letter.

For an anchor on what this work genuinely costs to perform, the Mortgage Bankers Association runs the most detailed benchmarking study in the industry. According to MBA's Servicing Operations Study and Forum, reported in July 2025, fully loaded servicing costs on a performing loan averaged $176 per loan in 2024. On a non-performing loan, $1,573 per loan.

What MBA measured (2024)Annual cost per loanRoughly per month
Performing loan$176About $15
Non-performing loan$1,573About $131

The monthly column is just the annual figure divided by twelve, shown to make the scale legible. Two things jump out.

First, professionals with scale, systems and dedicated compliance staff spend about $15 a month per loan to do this properly. If you planned to do the same job free in a spreadsheet, you were not pricing your own time, and you were not pricing the compliance layer at all.

Second, look at the gap. Servicing a defaulted loan costs roughly nine times what servicing a performing one costs. That is not because the mailbox got heavier. It is because default is where the timelines, the notices, the loss mitigation review, the foreclosure referral rules and the legal exposure all live at once. That gap is the risk you are outsourcing.

So the honest framing is not "servicing costs me a piece of my yield." It is: you pay a modest, predictable fee to convert an unbounded, unpredictable legal liability into a line item. Underwrite it from day one, like taxes or title, and the yield question answers itself.

How to Pick One

Not all servicers are the same, and the cheap one is not automatically the wrong one. Things worth asking before you board a loan:

  • What states are you licensed in, and can I see the NMLS numbers? If the answer is vague, stop there.
  • Do you service non-performing loans, or only performing? Plenty of shops only want clean paper. Find out before you buy a defaulted note, not after.
  • Do you handle escrow, or is that on me? Escrowed and non-escrowed loans are different products.
  • What is the full fee schedule, including add-ons? The monthly number is rarely the whole number.
  • How fast do you board a loan? Boarding delays cost you payments.
  • Can you remit directly to my IRA custodian? If the note lives in a retirement account, this is not optional.
  • What does the borrower see? The welcome letter is your borrower's first impression. A clean handoff makes a struggling borrower far more likely to pick up the phone later.

That last one gets underrated. Most of a non-performing workout comes down to whether the borrower is willing to talk to you. A confused borrower who got a Zelle request from a stranger is not going to be.

The Real Point: Servicing Is What Makes It Passive

Circle back to why you wanted notes. The landlord's problem was never the money. It was that the income came bolted to a job. The tenant, the turn, the capex, the 11pm call. You bought the position instead of the property to get the cashflow without the job.

Self-collecting hands the job right back. Different uniform, same job. Instead of chasing a tenant, you are tracking payment dates, running escrow math, watching a 120 day clock, writing payoff statements on a seven business day deadline, and personally holding an FDCPA exposure you did not have to hold. To save fifteen bucks.

The servicer is not a cost center you tolerate. It is the piece of infrastructure that makes the sentence "notes are passive" actually true instead of aspirational. It is the boring line item that keeps the asset boring, and boring is the entire point.

Pay the fee. Underwrite it up front. Go find the next note.

So what is actually holding you back on this one, the cost, or just not knowing which servicer to call first?
